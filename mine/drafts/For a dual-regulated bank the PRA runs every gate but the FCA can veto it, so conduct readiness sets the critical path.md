# For a dual-regulated bank the PRA runs every gate but the FCA can veto it, so conduct readiness sets the critical path

For a dual-regulated bank the PRA leads every gate: authorisation, new permissions, senior managers and change in control. But the FCA has to consent to the main ones, and consent means a veto. A product that needs a new regulated activity or a new senior manager can't go faster than the FCA's conduct assessment, even though the PRA makes the decision. Plans that treat the PRA as the only gatekeeper will underestimate lead time.

## Why I think this
- The PRA is lead regulator for dual-regulated firms and runs a single administrative process ([[raw/fca-boe-memorandum-of-understanding-2024.pdf#page=6]]).
- Before granting a permission, or varying one by adding a regulated activity, the PRA will "obtain consent from the FCA" ([[raw/fca-boe-memorandum-of-understanding-2024.pdf#page=21]]). Under the MoU, consent means "the power to veto", and disagreements "must be resolved before the decision is made" ([[raw/fca-boe-memorandum-of-understanding-2024.pdf#page=21]]).
- A new deposit-taker is authorised only if both regulators are satisfied on their own Threshold Conditions ([[raw/pra-approach-banking-supervision-2023.pdf#page=42]]). Each regulator assesses against its own conditions ([[raw/fca-boe-memorandum-of-understanding-2024.pdf#page=21]]).
- PRA-designated senior managers need FCA consent, and the FCA alone approves customer-facing ones such as compliance and AML ([[raw/fca-boe-memorandum-of-understanding-2024.pdf#page=21]], [[raw/fca-boe-memorandum-of-understanding-2024.pdf#page=22]]).
- So whichever regulator is slower sets the timeline. For a product change, that is likely to be the FCA's conduct view, because conduct is what the product changes (reasoning). The sources give no processing times for either regulator, so which one is actually slower is untested (reasoning).
