# The FCA's consumer protection objective is a calibration rule, so product design should vary protection by risk and customer segment

The statute doesn't ask for maximum protection. It asks for "an appropriate degree", and it tells the FCA to weigh product risk, customer capability and consumer responsibility against firms' duty of care. A lending, cards or payments product that applies the same safeguards to every segment is probably over-protecting simple, low-risk journeys and under-protecting high-risk ones. The defensible design is to scale disclosure, friction and care with risk and capability.

## Why I think this
- The objective is "securing an appropriate degree of protection for consumers" ([[raw/uk-parliament-fsma-2000-part-1a.md]], s. 1C(1)).
- The FCA must weigh the "differing degrees of risk" of different transactions and consumers' "differing degrees of experience and expertise" ([[raw/uk-parliament-fsma-2000-part-1a.md]], s. 1C(2)(a)–(b)).
- It must weigh both that "consumers should take responsibility for their decisions" and that providers owe "a level of care that is appropriate" to the risk and "the capabilities of the consumers in question" ([[raw/uk-parliament-fsma-2000-part-1a.md]], s. 1C(2)(d)–(e)).
- Proportionality, meaning burdens proportionate to benefits, binds both regulators ([[raw/uk-parliament-fsma-2000-part-1a.md]], s. 3B(1)(b)).
- Payment services and e-money are named as regulated financial services, so this objective reaches payments products directly ([[raw/uk-parliament-fsma-2000-part-1a.md]], s. 1H(2)(f)–(g)).
- Taken together, the statute frames protection as proportionate to risk and capability, not uniform (reasoning). Product rules made under it, such as conduct rules, may be stricter than this framing; this source doesn't show them (reasoning).
