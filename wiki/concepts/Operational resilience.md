# Operational resilience

The ability of firms, and of the financial sector as a whole, to prevent, adapt, respond to, recover from and learn from operational disruptions ([[raw/pra-approach-banking-supervision-2023.pdf#page=36]]).

## What the sources say
- **A prudential matter.** The PRA expects firms to observe high standards in managing operational as well as financial risks. For example, firms should have processes to keep their important business services running in financial stress or failure ([[raw/pra-approach-banking-supervision-2023.pdf#page=36]]).
- **Disruption is assumed.** The PRA's approach assumes that disruptions will occur from time to time and leave firms unable to provide their services for a period ([[raw/pra-approach-banking-supervision-2023.pdf#page=36]]).
- **Impact tolerances.** The PRA's primary focus is the important business services that customers and the wider economy rely on. Firms set impact tolerances and must be able to stay within them in a wide range of severe but plausible scenarios ([[raw/pra-approach-banking-supervision-2023.pdf#page=36]]).
- **Proportionate assurance.** The PRA seeks more assurance on impact tolerances from firms with a higher potential impact ([[raw/pra-approach-banking-supervision-2023.pdf#page=36]]).
- **Board responsibility.** A firm's operational resilience is the responsibility of its board, with clear lines of accountability through responsibility for the firm's internal operations and technology ([[raw/pra-approach-banking-supervision-2023.pdf#page=36]]).
- **In the PRA's risk assessment.** Operational resilience is one of the mitigating factors the PRA weighs against a firm's gross risk ([[raw/pra-approach-banking-supervision-2023.pdf#page=18]], [[raw/pra-approach-banking-supervision-2023.pdf#page=19]]). It also feeds the firm's [[wiki/concepts/Proactive Intervention Framework]] stage ([[raw/pra-approach-banking-supervision-2023.pdf#page=44]]).
- **Business continuity rules (FCA).** A firm must take reasonable steps to ensure continuity and regularity in its regulated activities (SYSC 4.1.6R). It must keep an adequate business continuity policy so that, if its systems are interrupted, losses are limited, essential data and functions are preserved and regulated activities continue, or are recovered in good time (SYSC 4.1.7R). Both are as the FCA quoted them for 2015–2018 ([[raw/fca-final-notice-tsb-2022.pdf#page=113]], [[raw/fca-final-notice-tsb-2022.pdf#page=114]], Annex A 2.11–2.12).
- **A case: TSB, 2018.** One failed IT migration led to an FCA penalty of £29.75m for breaching [[wiki/concepts/Principles for Businesses]] 2 and 3 ([[raw/fca-final-notice-tsb-2022.pdf#page=1]], para 1.1; [[raw/fca-final-notice-tsb-2022.pdf#page=10]], para 2.33). The FCA found TSB's incident management and business continuity "insufficiently robust and ineffective": it had planned for incidents lasting hours or days, not weeks ([[raw/fca-final-notice-tsb-2022.pdf#page=96]], para 5.6(d)). The FCA also took account of a PRA penalty proposed for the same events ([[raw/fca-final-notice-tsb-2022.pdf#page=100]], para 6.18). That the PRA framed it as a safety-and-soundness failure is AI only ([[raw/claude-2026-09-27-foundations-1-1-who-regulates-what.md]] · AI). See [[wiki/entities/TSB Bank]].
